So, this is Back Them at Home. Seven simple ways to support your child in anything they work hard at.

None of them cost money. None of them need you to know anything about the sport, the instrument or the exam. And you can start most of them tonight.

### 3. Who I am  (1:20, camera: bubble)

I'm a rower. In 2024 I won Olympic gold in the men's eight in Paris. Before that I was World Champion twice and European Champion five times.

I'm not telling you that to show off. Honestly, the medal is the least interesting part. How I got there is far less impressive than you'd think. I wasn't spotted early. I didn't follow a plan. And I nearly didn't make it at all.

That's the bit that's useful to you.

### 4. The fast kid  (1:53, camera: bubble)

Growing up, I was the fast kid. My dad coached me, and I loved sport from as early as I can remember. I won sports day. I ran the hundred metres at the nationals. On the rugby pitch, I was quicker than everyone.

And talent did all the work for me. I never had to listen much, or practise the boring bits, because I was already winning. Everyone told me how talented I was, and I believed them.

That turned out to be a problem. Talent was the only thing I'd built.

### 5. Then everyone caught up  (2:32, camera: bubble)

In my teens, it all changed. I got Osgood Schlatter's in both knees, so I was in pain most of the time. Then everyone else hit puberty. They got taller, stronger and faster, and I didn't keep up.

I went from the A team to the B team at Millfield, in front of my whole squad. I'm dyslexic, and my A levels didn't go to plan either. I didn't really know who I was anymore.

If you've got a child who's an early developer, or a late one, hold on to this. Where they are at twelve tells you very little about where they'll be at twenty five.

### 6. Rowing at 18  (3:17, camera: bubble)

At eighteen, I was off contact sport with a rugby injury. Some friends were rowing, so I gave it a go. It was an hour each way to get to the water.

In my first ever two kilometre test, I went off far too fast and was sick behind a hedge. Not a great start.

But I liked the people, and I kept turning up. Nobody picked me out. Nobody had a plan for me. I just kept going back.

### 7. The seventh boat  (3:51, camera: bubble)

I went to Oxford Brookes, after a foundation year because of those A levels. I started in the seventh boat. Seventh. Then I moved up, boat by boat, to the first boat. We won at Henley. I rowed for Great Britain under 23s. I got a first class degree, and I worked at Williams F1 while doing a part time master's, still trying to make the national team. And I did.

None of that was talent. It was showing up. Listening to feedback. Being patient when progress was slow. Character, built quietly, one session at a time.

### 8. Tokyo 2021  (4:32, camera: bubble)

Then came Tokyo. The Games were put back a year because of COVID. I had my tonsils out, and afterwards I developed health anxiety. I couldn't sleep properly. I started thinking about results instead of the job in front of me. I worried about selection. And I didn't tell anyone how I was really feeling.

In the Olympic final, we nearly crashed into the Italian boat. We came fourth. I'm proud I was there, but I wasn't the athlete I could have been.

And here's why I'm telling you. It wasn't my talent, and it wasn't my fitness. I'd lost the habits that built me. Talking. Focusing on what I could control. Being honest about how I felt.

### 9. Paris 2024  (5:21, camera: bubble)

After Tokyo, I did the work. I had therapy, and I finally understood how my body reacted to stress. It took about six months to love rowing again. Then we became World Champions, and World Champions again the next year.

Those three years were different. I talked more. I focused on the process, not the result.

On the morning of the Olympic final in Paris, I ate a bowl of porridge and sat quietly for forty five minutes. I'd noticed a sore hamstring, and I let it go. I said to myself, it doesn't matter, I'm ready. And I was. That calm wasn't luck. It had been built, over three years of small choices.

We won gold.

### 10. The big idea  (6:10, camera: bubble)

So here's the one line I want you to remember from tonight.

Talent opens the door. Character decides how far you walk through it.

Talent made me fast as a kid. Character made me an Olympic champion. And talent is mostly luck. Character can be built, by anyone, at any age. That's good news for every child, and every parent watching.

### 11. Character is built at home  (6:36, camera: bubble)

And this is the bit I really want you to hear. Character isn't mostly built on the pitch or in the music room. It's built at home. In the car. At the kitchen table. In the chat at ten o'clock at night when they can't sleep before a test.

Coaches and teachers get them for a few hours a week. You get the rest.

And you don't need to know anything about rowing, or the violin, or GCSE chemistry. A match, a piano exam, a school play and a maths test all feel the same to a child. Big moment. People watching. Scared of letting someone down. So everything I'm about to say works for all of it.

### 12. Seven things that help  (7:25, camera: bubble)

So here are seven things that help at home. The ride home. Big moments. Fuel and sleep. Goals they control. Their dream. Support without pressure. And letting them struggle.

No need to write them down. At the end there's one slide with all seven to screenshot, and it's in your email too.

### 13. 1. The ride home  (7:48, camera: bubble)

Number one. The ride home.

Two American coaches, Bruce Brown and Rob Miller, spent around thirty years asking college athletes about their parents. Many told them one of their worst memories of sport was the car journey home. The replay. The questions. The advice.

When they asked what their parents said that made them feel great, the answer was simple. Six words. I love to watch you play.

When your child gets in the car, they're still in it. Heart racing. Maybe embarrassed, maybe angry, maybe just starving. It's not the moment for feedback. Even good feedback.

### 14. 1. Try saying  (8:29, camera: bubble)

So, talk after, not during. Let the feelings settle first.

Start with something like, I loved watching you today. Then ask an open question. What did you enjoy? Or, do you want to talk about it now, or later?

Let them lead. If they want your view, they'll ask. If they don't want to talk at all, that's fine. Put the radio on. Get them some food. You being there says more than anything you could add.

### 15. 2. Big moments  (9:02, camera: bubble)

Number two. Tests and big moments.

To a child, a cup final, a grade five piano exam, a mock and an audition all feel the same. Something matters, and people will see how it goes.

Nerves are normal. Usually they just mean your child cares. *(Rory to check)* I had nerves before every big race. In Tokyo, I let them run the show. By Paris, I'd learned to notice them and let them go. The feeling is the body getting ready. It isn't a sign that something's wrong.

### 16. 2. Try this  (9:39, camera: bubble)

Three things help.

First, keep the routine boring. Bag packed early. Same kind of dinner. Same bedtime. My Olympic final started with porridge and sitting quietly.

Second, give the feeling a name. That's your body getting ready. Nerves and excitement feel almost the same inside, so some people find it helps to say, I'm excited, instead of, I'm nervous.

Third, perspective, and say it before, not after. Whatever happens, I'm proud of how you've prepared. That lifts the weight of your opinion off their shoulders, so they can get on with it.

### 17. 3. Fuel and sleep  (10:18, camera: bubble)

Number three. Fuel and sleep. The least exciting point, and maybe the most important.

In the national team, I slept around twelve hours a night. Sleep was part of training.

For children, sleep experts recommend nine to twelve hours a night for ages six to twelve, and eight to ten hours for teenagers. A tired child finds everything harder. Learning, training, managing their mood. Including being nice to you.

### 18. 3. Try this  (10:48, camera: bubble)

You don't need a meal plan. Keep it simple.

The biggest one for most families is phones. Try charging them outside the bedroom. For everyone, you included.

Regular meals, especially breakfast before a big day, and a water bottle in the bag. And protect the routine, not just one early night before a match.

If you're ever worried about your child's eating or sleep, speak to your GP. This is just the everyday stuff that helps.

### 19. 4. Goals they control  (11:20, camera: bubble)

Number four. Goals they control.

Most children set goals like, I want to win, or, I want a grade seven. That's fine as a direction. But they can't control it. Someone else might be better on the day.

When I started in the seventh boat, if my only goal had been the Olympics, I'd have given up. Instead it was the next boat. Then the next session. Then one thing in my technique.

This is one of the cards from my Athlete Deck. A goal gives you direction. A system gets you there.

### 20. 4. Try this  (11:59, camera: bubble)

So try this. Once a week, ask them for one small goal that's completely in their control. Practise scales for ten minutes every day. Ask the coach one question at training. Be in bed by half nine four nights this week.

Write it down and stick it on the fridge. Then check it together on Sunday. Not as a test. Just, how did it go? What's next?

Small steps, written down, checked together. That's how I got from the seventh boat to the Olympics.

### 21. 5. Their dream  (12:35, camera: bubble)

Number five. Their future, their dream.

Lots of us have a dream for our children. A scholarship. A county place. A particular school. That comes from love. But it only really works if it's their dream.

Nobody planned rowing for me. I found it at eighteen, by accident, because I was free to try something new. If someone had decided at twelve that I was a rugby player and that was that, I'd never have sat in a boat.

Let them try lots of things. Let them change their mind.

### 22. 5. Try asking  (13:13, camera: bubble)

So ask, and then really listen. What do you love about it? What would you like to get better at?

And the brave one. How can I help, and is there anything I do that doesn't help?

You might not love every answer. But if they know their dream is safe with you, they'll keep telling you about it. That's worth more than any scholarship.

### 23. 6. Support, not pressure  (13:41, camera: bubble)

Number six. Support without pressure.

Children watch your face more than the scoreboard. When they come off the pitch or out of the exam hall, they look at you to find out how it went. Your reaction is the mirror they look into.

If you only light up when they win, they learn that winning is what makes you happy. If you light up when they've worked hard and been a good teammate, they learn that's what matters.

### 24. 6. Swap this for this  (14:14, camera: bubble)

So praise the things they control. Effort, courage, kindness, how they handled a mistake.

Instead of, you're so talented, try, you worked so hard for that. Instead of, well done for winning, try, I loved how you kept going after that mistake. Instead of, why didn't you, try, what did you learn?

I was told I was talented my whole childhood. It didn't help me one bit when things got hard.

### 25. 7. Let them struggle  (14:44, camera: bubble)

Number seven. Challenge them.

Kindness and high standards go together. You can love them completely and still expect them to do hard things.

When I was dropped to the B team, I sulked for a bit. Then I went back to the coach and asked one question. What's the one thing I need to fix first? He told me. I worked on it. That question changed everything. Nobody rescued me, and I'm grateful. The struggle is where the growth happens.

### 26. 7. Try this  (15:18, camera: bubble)

So when they're stuck, wait before you rescue. Count to ten in your head. Then ask, what do you think you could try?

Let them email the coach themselves. Let them sort out their own kit. Let them have a go at the hard maths before you jump in.

And hold the standard, warmly. I know this is hard. I also know you can do hard things. A little struggle now, with you right beside them, is the best training there is.
